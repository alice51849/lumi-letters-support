How to teach your child the ABCs: letter names, letter sounds and tracing

Knowing the alphabet song is a lovely start, but reading needs more: for every letter a child learns its name, the sound it makes and how to write it. Name, sound, shape: the three things to learn for each letter

Children often know letter names long before they know letter sounds. Both matter, but the sound is what they will use to read words, so give it equal time.

LetterNameSoundExample word
A a“ay”/a/ as in appleapple
B b“bee”/b/ as in ballball
C c“see”/k/ as in catcat
M m“em”/m/ as in moonmoon
S s“ess”/s/ as in sunsun
T t“tee”/t/ as in toptop

Say sounds short and clean: /m/ is “mmm”, not “muh”. Adding an extra vowel to every sound makes blending harder later (“buh-a-tuh” does not sound like “bat”).

A 10-minute daily routine

Short and daily beats long and weekly. Pick one or two letters a week and repeat this routine:

  1. Hear it (2 minutes). Say the letter name and its sound, then three words that start with it. Ask your child to repeat.
  2. Find it (2 minutes). Hunt for the letter on cereal boxes, signs and book covers. Point to both the uppercase and the lowercase form.
  3. Trace it (3 minutes). Trace the uppercase letter, then the lowercase one, starting from the top and following the stroke order.
  4. Play with it (3 minutes). One sound game from the list below, then stop while it is still fun.

Review old letters for a minute before adding new ones. When a child can say the sound of a letter without looking at a picture, that letter is ready to move into games.

Tracing tips: stroke order, big movements, uppercase then lowercase

  • Start at the top. Almost every letter begins at the top. Learning the right start point early keeps handwriting neat and fast later.
  • Go big first. Trace in the air, in sand or with a finger on a screen before using a pencil. Big movements build the motor memory for small ones.
  • Pair the two forms. Practise “B” and “b” on the same day so your child links them as one letter with one sound.
  • Watch the mirror letters. b and d, p and q are easy to mix up at this age. Say the sound while tracing: the stroke and the sound together help them stick.

Sound games you can play anywhere

  • First sounds: “I spy something that starts with /s/.”
  • Ending sounds: “What sound do you hear at the end of cat?”
  • Middle vowels: “Is it c-a-t or c-u-t?” Stretch the middle sound.
  • Big and small: write a few uppercase letters, and ask your child to match the lowercase partners.
  • Missing letter: write A B _ D and ask what is missing, to practise letter order.

These are the same kinds of skills that build a foundation for early reading: hearing sounds in words, knowing letter order and connecting each sound to its shape.
